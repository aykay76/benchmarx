Platform engineering

Treat the platform as a product.

An internal platform that nobody chose to use is just infrastructure with a roadmap. The platforms that last treat developers as customers, and adoption as the measure of success.

4 min read

Two ways platforms go wrong

Platforms that are run as projects tend to fail in one of two directions.

  • The ticket queue. Every change goes through the platform team, who become the bottleneck they were created to remove.
  • The ivory tower. A technically impressive system designed from the architecture down, and then mandated, rather than designed from the developer's problem up.

Both come from the same root: nobody asked who the customers are and what is actually slowing them down.

Start with friction, grow from evidence

A useful rule is to build the thinnest platform that removes the most painful friction, and let usage tell you what to build next.

Project thinking versus product thinking

As a projectAs a product
Success isScope deliveredTeams choosing it, and getting faster
UsersTold to adopt itChoose to adopt it
The roadmap comes fromThe target architectureObserved developer friction
Support looks likeA queue of ticketsSelf-service, docs and a feedback loop
Ownership endsAt launchNever, until it's retired

If teams have to be ordered to use it, the platform hasn't yet earned its place. Mandates can follow adoption. They shouldn't be a substitute for it.

Where this gets hard

What surprises teams after the launch

Early adopters forgive rough edges. The mainstream teams compare you with whatever they already have, and they're right to. Winning them means reliability, good documentation and an honest migration story, not enthusiasm.

Each exception is reasonable, and each one erodes the standard that makes the platform valuable. You need principled ways to deviate, and clarity about what a team takes on when it does.

If common changes need a person on the platform team, you've rebuilt the ticket queue. Self-service takes real investment in interfaces, defaults and documentation, and it's what lets the team scale without growing in proportion.

Budgets and headcount tied to a delivery date make it hard to staff the long tail: support, upgrades and deprecations. A platform needs a standing team and an owner for as long as it exists.
