Platform engineering

Running it, and proving it helps.

A platform is a service with customers. It needs the same things as any service: objectives, telemetry and someone on the hook. And it needs evidence that delivery is actually getting better.

5 min read

Hold the platform to a service standard

If a team's release depends on your pipeline, then your pipeline's reliability is their reliability. The platform should have its own objectives, such as how often builds succeed and how long it takes to provision an environment. It should also have an on-call rota, incident reviews and honest status communication.

Teams forgive a platform that is sometimes down. They don't forgive one that is down without telling them.

Telemetry you own, from day one

The paved path should emit logs, metrics and traces by default, using open standards such as OpenTelemetry, and send them to backends you control. That gives every service a baseline view without anyone instrumenting it by hand, and it keeps your operational data portable.

It also needs discipline. Telemetry has a cost, and unbounded detail can end up costing more than the system it describes.

Prove the value

Speed

Lead time for changes, and how long a new service takes to reach production for the first time.

Stability

Change failure rate, and how quickly teams recover when something breaks.

Adoption

How many services use the path, and how many chose to rather than being told to.

Developer experience

How teams feel about the platform, and how much time still goes on toil.

Measure outcomes for teams

Tickets closed and features shipped measure the platform team's activity. The question that matters is whether the teams it serves are delivering faster and more safely than before.

Where this gets hard

What surprises teams after the launch

Once a number is used to judge people, it gets gamed. Treat delivery metrics as prompts for conversation with the teams concerned, and read them alongside what those teams actually say.

It's hard to show improvement if you never measured the "before". Capturing where teams started, even roughly, is one of the cheapest and most valuable things you can do early.

Shared platforms blur cost. Without sensible allocation and defaults that prevent waste, such as idle environments left running, spend grows quietly and the platform becomes the easy target in a budget review.

Every platform change eventually has to reach every consumer. Treat breaking changes like a product release, with migration help and a realistic deprecation timeline, or you'll accumulate a long tail of unsupported versions.
