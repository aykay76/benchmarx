Platform engineering

Golden paths, not gates.

The best platforms don't stop people doing the wrong thing. They make the right thing the easiest thing, so that following the standard is also the quickest route.

4 min read

What a golden path is

A golden path is an opinionated, supported route from an idea to software running in production. It bundles the decisions most teams would otherwise make, and remake, for themselves: how to structure a repository, build and test, ship, observe and operate.

It is a path, not a fence. Teams can step off it, but the path should be good enough that few want to.

From idea to production

Three properties that make it work

Good defaults

The compliant, supportable choice is already made, and it's the one teams get without thinking.

Self-service

No ticket, no waiting for a person. If it needs a human to say yes, it's a gate.

Honest escape hatches

Teams can leave the path with a clear understanding that they now own what they leave.

Gates have their place

Some things really are non-negotiable, such as access to production data or changes that affect other teams. Those can be enforced. But a platform made mostly of gates turns developers into applicants, and teams will route around anything that treats them that way.

A useful test: would a team choose this route if nobody made them? If not, fix the route before adding enforcement.

Where this gets hard

What surprises teams after the launch

A template copied two years ago is now a fork. The harder question is how improvements reach the services that already exist, without breaking them and without relying on every team to notice.

Too rigid and teams leave, building shadow platforms of their own. Too loose and you've provided no leverage. Finding the right level takes evidence from real teams, and it will move over time.

Scaffolding a service is the easy day. Upgrading, migrating and deprecating across hundreds of services over years is where platforms succeed or quietly decay.

Docs that were right at launch won't stay right unless they live next to the code, are tested like code, and have someone responsible for them.
